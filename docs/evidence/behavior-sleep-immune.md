# Behavioural health, sleep, cognition and the immune system

Evidence file for VESTA (Vital Evaluation & Self-Triage Assistant). Compiled 2026-11-14 with live web access.
Peer-reviewed items were retrieved from PubMed (abstract or PMC full text) and are linked by DOI. NASA standards and reports were read from the PDFs linked below.
Confidence: **high** = number read directly in the primary source; **medium** = read in a NASA secondary summary, or an interpretation of a primary number; **low** = indirect evidence only.

## Measurement in space

**Sleep duration and timing**
- On ISS: wrist actigraphy with a light sensor plus sleep logs (the largest study logged daily on the ground and 1 week in every 3 in flight); the Reaction Self-Test also collects self-reported sleep times.
- Self-measure: on waking, log lights-out time, final wake time, night awakenings, sleep quality (0 to 100), any sleep medication (name, dose, clock time) and caffeine taken late in the shift; let a wearable supply total sleep time if one is worn.
- Cadence: every day; show last night and a 7-day mean.

**Alertness: PVT-B / Reaction Self-Test**
- On ISS: Windows laptop software; 3 minutes; ISS research cadence was every 4 days in flight (24 astronauts); Mars-500 used it weekly.
- Self-measure: restrain yourself at the same device, respond as fast as possible when the counter starts, avoid pressing early; record hours awake, last sleep and stimulant use with each test (the Cognition survey does this); do not take a baseline test within 1 hour of waking, after more than 16 hours awake, or when sick. Keep the same hardware, because reaction times differ between devices.
- Cadence (proposed): every 3 to 4 days, plus before safety-critical tasks after short sleep. Implement with 2 to 5 s inter-stimulus intervals and a 355 ms lapse threshold to match the flight version.

**Cognition battery**
- 10 tests, median 17.8 minutes, 15 versions, feedback as a 0 to 1,000 score; used on ISS for research and listed by its developers as part of NASA's Behavioral Health and Performance Standard Measures. Practice effects differ by test, so raw scores cannot be colour-coded without the normative correction. Not suitable for self-triage.

**WinSCAT**
- Operational requirement: 11 to 15 minutes, baseline from 6 months before launch (6 pre-flight sessions), once per month in orbit, and again after any medical event such as head injury, decompression sickness, toxic gas exposure or medication side effects. Off-nominal scores are interpreted by the crew surgeon in context; no public numeric cut-off.

**Mood, stress and behaviour**
- On ISS: private psychological conference every 2 weeks for at least 15 minutes; weekly private medical conference with the crew surgeon; personal journals (used by NASA both as an outlet and as research data). Analogs: Mars-500 ran a weekly set of Beck Depression Inventory-II, Profile of Mood States short form, a conflict questionnaire, PVT-B and visual analogue scales for stress and fatigue; Antarctic stations used a monthly Mental Health Checklist.
- Self-measure: three quick daily ratings (mood, stress, fatigue or workload) as trend lines; PHQ-4 once a week or once a fortnight (its wording covers the last 2 weeks); a free-text journal entry; note any conflict and whether it was within the crew or with the ground.
- Cadence: daily ratings, weekly or fortnightly PHQ-4 timed a day or two before the private psychological conference so results can be discussed.

**Immune and skin signs**
- On ISS: research sampling only (saliva for EBV, VZV and HSV-1; urine for CMV; blood returned to Earth within 48 hours). No routine on-board viral testing was found.
- Self-measure: weekly head-to-toe skin check; for any rash log the site, size, redness, itch or pain, a photo, the day it started and the response to treatment; also log cold sores, tingling or band-like pain, allergy symptoms (sneezing, itchy eyes, rhinitis), cold symptoms, and how any cut or abrasion is healing. NASA attributes cold sores, shingles, rashes, atypical allergies and infections reported on the ISS to altered immunity.
- Cadence: weekly check plus event-driven entries.

**Body temperature**
- On ISS (research): a non-invasive forehead "Double Sensor" combining skin temperature and heat flux, sampled at rest and during exercise before flight, on flight days 15, 45, 75, 105, 135 and 165, and after landing.
- Self-measure (proposed): same thermometer and same site every time, at rest and before exercise, at a consistent time of day; record recent exercise. Build a personal in-flight baseline through the first 75 days, because that is when the rise plateaued.
- Cadence (proposed): weekly when well; every few hours when unwell or after a Watch reading.

**Light and noise in the sleep area**
- Vehicle or habitat sensors, not a crew self-measure. VESTA can show the requirement values (BSI-07, BSI-08) as context when sleep is poor.

## Caveats

- **Proposed bands are not guidelines.** Every threshold cell tagged (P) is my derivation. No source in this domain defines Watch, Act or Urgent tiers, and no NASA numeric flight rule for sleep hours, PVT results, mood scores or fever was found. A flight surgeon should approve the (P) bands before use.
- **Core temperature and fever.** The 1 °C rise comes from one study of 11 astronauts using a research forehead heat-flux sensor. I found no replication with oral or ear thermometers and no NASA statement on an in-flight fever threshold. The safe reading is "a fixed 38.0 °C cut-off may mislead in either direction; compare with a personal in-flight baseline", not "fever starts at 39 °C in space".
- **PVT-B timing differs between sources.** The 2011 validation paper states 1 to 4 s inter-stimulus intervals (including a 1 s feedback period) on a handheld device; the 2015 Cognition paper states the flight version uses 2 to 5 s. The 355 ms lapse threshold was derived post hoc and the authors say it may depend on hardware.
- **Meaningful change on the PVT-B is not defined for individuals.** Published results are group effect sizes. The personal-baseline rule in the thresholds table is a statistical convention, not a validated alert.
- **Sleep figures differ by method.** Actigraphy gives 6.09 h on ISS; self-report in a later ISS cohort gives about 6.5 h. Sleep deficiency also began before launch (6.41 h about 3 months before flight), so in-flight values are not purely a space effect.
- **Herpesvirus numbers are internally inconsistent.** The 2019 review says 14 of 23 ISS astronauts (61%) shed "one or more" viruses but also that 96% shed EBV; the underlying 2017 study reports only 1 of 23 shedding nothing, so 61% is most likely the CMV figure. The 2017 study found no HSV-1 reactivation, while the review reports HSV-1 DNA in 8% of saliva samples. BSI-27 uses the per-virus figures only.
- **Third-quarter phenomenon.** Journal data support a morale dip; NASA still calls phase effects an open question, and Concordia data show stress rising progressively with interpersonal stress peaking near the end. Do not hard-code a third-quarter warning as fact.
- **Antarctic incidence.** The evidence report gives both "12.5% presented with symptoms meeting criteria" and "an overall incidence rate of 5.2%" for the same study; I could not resolve which denominator applies, hence medium confidence for BSI-20. Resolved at verification: the abstract of the underlying study (Palinkas 2004, https://doi.org/10.3402/ijch.v63i2.17702) says 39 of 313 debriefed crew (12.5%) met criteria for one or more DSM-IV disorders, and that the incidence was 5.2% after weighting for the low participation of civilian personnel; mood disorders were 30.2% of all diagnoses.
- **PHQ instruments.** They are validated in primary care, not in astronauts, and rely on honest self-report in a group known to under-report. Their items refer to the last 2 weeks. The public-domain statement comes from the developer-hosted manual; one third-party database lists the PHQ-4 as "Not Free" while showing a cost of 0.00 USD.
- **Cognition battery duration.** The developers report a median of 17.8 minutes; NASA's 2025 evidence report says "up to 30 minutes".
- **Countermeasure strength varies.** Plants, virtual reality, journaling and care packages rest on observational, anecdotal or debrief evidence. The exercise and immune link is an association in 22 crew members. Nap benefit is from aviation, not spaceflight.
- **Not confirmed in the sources opened:** the cadence of private family conferences (often described as weekly); the total length and time-of-day schedule of the Reaction Self-Test; the widely quoted NASA nap figures (26 minutes, 34% and 54% improvements); the colour-temperature settings of the ISS solid-state lights; numeric WinSCAT off-nominal criteria; any quantified link between cabin CO2 and sleep (the standard only sets a 1-hour average limit of 3 mmHg, and NASA's brief lists CO2 as a contributor to behavioural decrements); HERA and SIRIUS symptom incidence figures.
- **Wound healing.** No human in-flight outcome data were found. Cell studies and animal unloading studies point in opposite directions, and reviewers note wound healing has not yet been a serious recorded problem for crews.
- **Microbial virulence.** The Salmonella result is one organism in a mouse model after culture in flight; it does not show that infections in crew are more severe.
- **Sleep-environment review figures.** The 35 dB, 17 to 28 and 40 to 60% humidity values are quoted by NASA's evidence report from a 2018 review that I did not open; the temperature unit was lost in text extraction and °C is assumed.
- **Reading method.** Several NASA web pages were read through an automated page summariser; all numbers taken from PDFs (both NASA-STD-3001 volumes, the 2025 evidence report, OCHMO-TB-016, the PHQ manual) were checked against extracted text. Of the peer-reviewed papers, full text was read for Stahn 2017, Basner 2011, Basner 2015 and Rooney 2019; for all others only the PubMed abstract was read. The 7-hour adult sleep recommendation is the stated recommendation of the Watson 2015 consensus statement, but the abstract opened does not restate the number.

### Conflicts with the clinical-thresholds file that the team must resolve

1. **Body temperature 38.0 to 39.3 °C with no symptoms**: Watch here, Act in the clinical-thresholds file. The 39.4 °C and 40.0 °C steps now agree in direction, but the other file uses 40.6 °C for "any reading" where this file uses 40.0 °C. Pick one rule set before coding.
2. **Meaning of "Urgent"**: this file uses Urgent for a PHQ-4 total of 9 to 12 and defines it as a same-day private conference; the clinical-thresholds file calls a same-day report "Act" and keeps Urgent for "act immediately", so it shows 9 to 12 as Act. Use one definition of each tier.
3. **Stress rating**: this file now notes the published 7.0 cut-off but does not colour by it; the clinical-thresholds file shows 7 or more as Act.
4. **PVT-B timing**: this file specifies the flight version (2 to 5 s intervals); the clinical-thresholds file quotes the 2011 handheld version (1 to 4 s). Implement one and say which.
5. **Rise above personal temperature baseline**: 1.0 °C here (a proposal). The clinical-thresholds file's "0.6 to 0.8 °C" figure was unsupported and has been removed there.

### Alarm-fatigue and permissiveness notes (no value changed)

- **T01**: even after the correction, the 7-day-mean rule will apply to a large share of crew, because the mean in-flight sleep is 6.09 h. The Act state should be raised once and then tracked, not repeated daily.
- **T02**: with about 6.1 h of sleep per 24 h, crew are awake for about 17.9 h on an ordinary day, so the Watch band (more than 16 h) will show late on most days. Consider showing it only when tasks are still scheduled.
- **T11**: in flight, peak exercise core temperature averaged 1.39 °C above pre-flight values and some crew passed 40 °C in short sub-maximal tests, so Watch or Act would appear in most sessions if a core sensor is worn.
- **T10**: this row has no low-temperature band; hypothermia (35.0 °C or less) is only in the clinical-thresholds file.
- **Watson 2015**: the "7 or more hours" wording was confirmed in the PubMed Central full text ("Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health"), https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716.
- **Editions**: NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1 Rev C (2023-09-15) and Volume 2 Rev F (2026-07-14) are the current revisions on NASA's standards page (https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo/human-spaceflight-and-aviation-standards/). The NASA evidence report is the 2025-02-10 edition.
