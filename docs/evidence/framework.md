# Five hazards, HRP risks and mission medical operations

Compiled 2026-11-14 for Void Voyagers. Web access was available; 58 pages and documents were opened (NASA, CSA, ESA, NSSDC, PubMed Central). NASA PDF briefs and NTRS documents were downloaded and their text extracted locally, so numbers from them were read directly.
Confidence key: **high** = number read in an opened primary or issuing-body source (or simple arithmetic on such a number, marked "derived"); **medium** = read in an opened source but the source is old (before 2018), is a poster or flattened table whose layout had to be interpreted, or disagrees with another source; **low** = from memory only (none of the numbered facts below are low).
**(D)** marks a design proposal for Void Voyagers that no source prescribes; it needs flight-surgeon sign-off.

## Thresholds

None for this domain: this file sets no clinical cut-offs (see the system-specific evidence files). Three sourced framework rules should still shape how the rule engine words Watch, Act and Urgent; how Void Voyagers applies them is a design proposal **(D)**.

- **Mission context decides who "Act" and "Urgent" can reach.** On the ISS a flight surgeon is reachable in real time and return is possible within about a day in an emergency, though the Crew-11 controlled return in January 2026 took 8 days (FRM-14, FRM-15). At the Moon voice is still near real-time but return takes 3–11 days (FRM-14). On a Mars mission there is no real-time help, a question and its answer are separated by up to about 44 minutes (2 × 22 minutes, derived from FRM-20), direct contact can be lost for 13–21 days (FRM-21) and evacuation is impossible (FRM-14). In Mars mode the first Urgent instruction therefore has to be something the crew can do on board, with "message the ground" as a parallel step, never the only step. **(D)**
- **Mission day changes what is expected.** Nausea, headache, congestion and back pain in the first 3 days are the norm, not an alarm (FRM-08); after about 2 weeks the concerns shift to muscle, aerobic fitness, bone, kidney stones and vision; after landing, balance and blood-pressure problems last hours to days ([OCHMO-TB-033](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-033-spaceflight-experience-medical-care.pdf)). A symptom rule should take mission day as an input. **(D)**
- **Routine-check cadence defines "overdue".** The ISS cadences in FRM-25 to FRM-29 (weekly, every 2 weeks, every 30 days, every 45 days, fixed eye-exam days) are operational schedules, not health thresholds; any Watch flag Void Voyagers raises for a missed check is a design choice. **(D)**

## Caveats

- **Crew Medical Officer training hours disagree.** A 2021 NASA abstract says about 15 hours; a 2017 NASA deck lists 7 + 26 hours plus an oddly wide "2–42 hours" of initial training; a 2023 NASA-authored paper says "a few days' worth". The often-quoted "40 hours" was not found in any source opened. Treat the CMO as lightly trained.
- **Number of CMOs.** NASA's 2025 brief says 2 per increment; a CSA page last modified in 2021 says 1 astronaut per mission. The NASA brief's role table also labels the role "Chief Medical Officer (CMO)", apparently a typo for Crew Medical Officer, since the description is of the onboard role.
- **Conference frequency.** NASA sources say the private medical conference is weekly; one CSA page says Canadian astronauts speak to their flight surgeon every 2 weeks while another CSA page says weekly. NASA-STD-3001 itself fixes no number: it leaves the frequency to the flight surgeon and the behavioural health provider.
- **Exercise time.** "At least 2 hours a day", "2 hours, 6 days a week", "up to 2 hours each day" (CSA) and 2.5 hours a day scheduled all appear; the last probably includes set-up time.
- **Mars figures in NASA's plain-language pages are rounded.** They give gravity as "about one-third" or "three-eighths" (data sheet: 0.38), the day as "37 minutes longer" (the sol is 39.6 minutes longer than 24 hours; 37 minutes is the sidereal figure) and the delay as "up to 20 minutes" (architecture paper: 21–23 minutes; geometry: 22.3 minutes).
- **Derived values.** Light times (Moon 1.2–1.4 s, Mars 3.0–22.3 minutes) and the 14.8-day lunar day and night were calculated from NSSDC distances and the synodic period. The Moon's poles, where Artemis landings are aimed, have a different light pattern that was not researched here.
- **Two-way delay figures.** The EIMO paper gives "up to 10 seconds" at the Moon and "up to 40 minutes" at Mars, which are operational planning values and do not equal twice the light time (2.6 s and about 44.6 minutes).
- **Time-to-return figures.** The 3–11-day lunar figure and the "days to weeks" Mars-preparatory figure come from a table whose text was flattened, so the column was inferred; the EIMO paper says "up to 2 weeks" for the Moon. "1 day or less" from the ISS is an emergency capability: the Crew-11 case in January 2026 took 8 days as a controlled return. The NASA pages opened do not say whether Crew-11 was the first medical evacuation from the station.
- **Risk counts and names differ by list.** 17 (public page), 22 (Human Research Roadmap), 29 or 30 (Human System Risk Board), and titles are worded differently in each. The hazard names also differ: HRP says "gravity fields", the board says "altered gravity".
- **The risk-to-hazard map is mostly inference.** Only the bone-fracture record was read for official primary and secondary hazards. No NASA table mapping every risk to a hazard was found.
- **Bone fracture after flight.** NASA's public page says fracture risk is not higher after return, but the 2025 risk record reports a higher relative rate of hip and spine fractures after long-duration flights (over 90–100 days).
- **SANS percentages use different definitions** (any swelling 70%, more-than-20-µm thickening 69%, disc edema 16%) and should not be averaged.
- **Gateway status is unsettled on NASA's own site.** The March 2026 release says Gateway is to be paused, yet the Gateway page (updated July 2026) still lists launch no earlier than 2027. Its orbital period and planned crew stays were not on the pages opened. Treat "Gateway" as a reference scenario, not a current plan.
- **Lunar surface stay length** for Artemis IV was not stated on the pages opened; only the reference-mission bands (under 30 days; 30 days to 1 year) are sourced.
- **Old or indirect cadence sources.** Monthly health status exams come from a 2006 abstract, the fitness evaluation cadence from a 2013 ESA astronaut logbook, and the 45-day hearing test from a 2017 deck (the hearing system has since changed). The current ISS medical requirement documents are not public.
- **Crew-time poster.** Minutes per activity were read from a chart; the label-to-number pairing was inferred, although the arithmetic is consistent (390 = 26 × 15 minutes; 195 = 13 × 15 minutes; 27,300 minutes = 2.5 hours × 182 days).
- **Not confirmed in an opened primary source:** cadence of body mass measurement, routine urinalysis and blood tests; device model names for the ultrasound, tonometer, blood analyser and ECG; reading schedule for dosimeters.
- **Fluid shift wording.** The brief says fluid redistribution gives a "12–15% reduction in whole body volume"; this is usually reported as a plasma-volume change, so the number is left out of the facts table.
- **NASA hazard pages are light on numbers.** The radiation page gives no doses; mission doses here come from a peer-reviewed NASA paper. NASA career dose limits were not researched in this file.
- **NASA-STD-3001 itself was not opened**; requirement text comes from the OCHMO technical briefs, which are derived from the standard and marked "for reference only".
- **The CSA "why science" page names no technology.** Bio-Monitor, Bio-Analyzer and the Vascular studies come from other CSA pages.
