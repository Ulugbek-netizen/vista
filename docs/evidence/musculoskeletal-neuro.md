# Bone, muscle, exercise, the sensorimotor system and SANS

Evidence file for Vista (crew health self-checks). Researched 2026-11-14 with live web access.

How to read this file:

- Every number below was read in a source opened during this research run. Journal abstracts and one full text were retrieved through PubMed; each is cited by its DOI link.
- Confidence: **high** = stated explicitly in a primary NASA / ESA / CSA document or a peer-reviewed paper; **medium** = primary source, but a small sample, a summary table, conference slides, or a clause that is an inference; **low** = not used.
- NASA Office of the Chief Health and Medical Officer (OCHMO) briefs say of themselves that they are "for reference only" and are not individual medical guidance. Vista should present them the same way.
- Nearly all human data come from about 6-month ISS missions in low Earth orbit. Nothing here has been validated for a 1,000+ day Mars mission.

| MSN-01 | Bone: loss rate by site, early ISS (before ARED) | In 14 ISS crewmembers on 4 to 6-month flights before the ARED was installed, DXA areal BMD fell 0.9% per month at the lumbar spine and 1.4-1.5% per month at the hip, while QCT showed hip trabecular volumetric BMD falling 2.2-2.7% per month and hip cortical volumetric BMD 0.4-0.5% per month. | [Lang et al. 2004, J Bone Miner Res](https://doi.org/10.1359/JBMR.040307) | high |
| MSN-02 | Bone: NASA summary rate | NASA's Human Research Program states that weight-bearing bones lose on average 1% to 1.5% of their mineral density per month during spaceflight. | [NASA, The Human Body in Space (updated 26 May 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space/) | high |
| MSN-03 | Bone: loss rate with current ISS countermeasures | NASA's 2025 review of exercise countermeasures summarises bone loss on 180+ day ISS missions with ARED, T2 and CEVIS exercise as averaging 0.5 to 1.0% of BMD per month, with pre-to-post-flight losses ranging from 2.1% to 8.5% depending on site and bone compartment. | [NASA/SP-20250000273, Table 7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250000273) | medium |
| MSN-04 | Bone: evidence that resistive exercise plus nutrition works | Among 13 ISS crewmembers who flew in 2006-2009, the 5 who trained on the ARED (loads up to 600 lbf) with adequate energy intake and vitamin D status returned with pelvis, femoral-neck, trochanter and total-hip BMD unchanged from preflight, unlike the 8 who used the older iRED (loads up to 297 lbf). | [Smith et al. 2012, J Bone Miner Res](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.1647) | high |
| MSN-05 | Bone: bisphosphonate study | Seven ISS astronauts who took 70 mg of oral alendronate once a week from 3 weeks before launch through a mean 5.5-month mission, in addition to ARED exercise, showed attenuation of essentially all measured bone changes (DXA BMD of spine, hip and pelvis, QCT hip trabecular and cortical bone mass, bone-resorption markers and urinary calcium) compared with 18 iRED users and 11 ARED-only users. | [LeBlanc et al. 2013, Osteoporos Int](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-012-2243-z) | high |
| MSN-06 | Bone: recovery time | In 45 long-duration crew members, BMD losses at landing averaged 2% to 9% across skeletal sites, and a fitted exponential model predicted that 50% of the lost bone would be restored within 9 months of landing. | [Sibonga et al. 2007, Bone](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bone.2007.08.022) | high |
| MSN-07 | Bone: recovery can be incomplete | Twelve months after landing, 9 of 17 astronauts had not fully recovered tibia total BMD, and those who flew longer than 6 months still had a median 3.9% deficit in tibia failure load compared with 0.4% after shorter missions. | [Gabel et al. 2022, Sci Rep](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-13461-1) | high |
| MSN-08 | Bone: what can be measured, and when | No bone fracture has occurred during a spaceflight mission so far, and NASA states that no system currently measures bone density in flight, so BMD is assessed by DXA only on the ground (for a 180-day ISS mission about 21-18 months and 180-30 days before launch, within 30 days after return and again at 1 year). | [NASA OCHMO-MTB-013 Rev D, Bone Loss (11 Feb 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf) | high |
| MSN-09 | Bone: kidney-stone implication | Bone resorption in weightlessness raises urinary calcium, and NASA's Integrated Medical Model ranks a kidney stone as the second most likely reason for an emergency medical evacuation from the ISS, although no US-segment crewmember has reported a stone in flight (NASA records 43 stone events in 29 crewmembers across pre-, post- and between-flight periods). | [NASA OCHMO-MTB-003 Rev A, Urinary Health (10 Mar 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ochmo-mtb-003-urinary-health.pdf) | high |
| MSN-10 | Muscle: calf loss on early ISS hardware | Nine ISS crewmembers who flew about 6 months with a treadmill, cycle and the early resistance device lost 13% of calf muscle volume (soleus 15%, gastrocnemius 10%) and 32% of calf peak power despite about 5 hours per week of aerobic exercise and resistance exercise 3-6 days per week. | [Trappe et al. 2009, J Appl Physiol](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.91578.2008) | high |
| MSN-11 | Muscle and fitness: loss with current ISS countermeasures | NASA's 2025 review reports that after 180+ days on the ISS with current exercise hardware, strength loss averages about 1% in the upper body and 9% in the lower body (ranges 1-3% and 4-12%) and VO2max falls 7.4% on average (range 2.35-11.21%). | [NASA/SP-20250000273, Table 7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250000273) | medium |
| MSN-12 | Exercise: it does not fully protect everyone | In 46 astronauts flying 178 +/- 48 days, about 600 minutes per week of aerobic and resistance exercise did not fully prevent deconditioning: individual pre-to-post-flight changes ranged from -30% to +5%, and an estimated up to 17% of astronauts would experience performance-limiting deconditioning if the same countermeasures were used on exploration missions. | [Scott et al. 2023, npj Microgravity](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41526-023-00256-5) | high |
| MSN-13 | NASA in-mission limits | NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1 requires in-mission countermeasures to keep aerobic capacity at or above 80% of the pre-mission value, skeletal muscle strength at or above 80% of baseline, and BMD at or above 95% of pre-mission at the hip and spine and 90% at the femoral neck. | [NASA OCHMO-TB-031, Exercise Overview (2 Oct 2023)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf) | high |
| MSN-14 | Exercise: ISS prescription | NASA's current ISS prescription is 2.5 hours per day, 6 days per week, made up of 30-60 minutes of resistive training on the ARED and on average 27 minutes of aerobic training on the CEVIS cycle or T2 treadmill, with the remaining time used for set-up and clean-up. | [NASA/SP-20250000273](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250000273) | high |
| MSN-15 | Exercise: Orion flywheel | Orion's flywheel exercise device weighs about 30 lb (about 14 kg), is roughly the size of a carry-on suitcase, gives up to 400 lb (about 180 kg) of resistance for rowing, squats and deadlifts and is used for 30 minutes per astronaut per day, whereas the ISS exercise machines together weigh more than 4,000 lb and occupy about 850 cubic feet. | [NASA, Life Encapsulated: Inside Orion for Artemis II (updated 15 Apr 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/life-encapsulated-inside-nasas-orion-for-artemis-ii-moon-mission/) | medium |
| MSN-16 | Exercise: exploration prototype (E4D) | ESA's European Enhanced Exploration Exercise Device (E4D) combines resistive training, cycling, rowing and rope pulling (more than 30 strength exercises) in one unit, is installed in the Columbus module of the ISS for a two-year evaluation, and was chosen by NASA as the exploration prototype on which a final recommendation will be made to the Artemis programme. | [ESA, E4D resistance training in space (8 Jul 2026)](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2026/07/Sophie_testing_E4D_s_resistance_training_in_space); [NASA NTRS 20240008204](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240008204) | high |
| MSN-17 | Exercise: partial gravity is an unknown | A systematic review of 43 partial-gravity studies (0.1-0.4 g, from Apollo data and ground simulations) found none that examined long-term effects and concluded that gravity below 0.4 g, which includes the Moon (about 1/6 g) and Mars (about 3/8 g), is probably not enough by itself to maintain musculoskeletal and cardiopulmonary condition, so exercise would still be needed. | [Richter et al. 2017, Front Physiol](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00583) | medium |
| MSN-18 | Spine: height increase | Seated height increased by about 6% from the preflight value in all 29 crewmembers measured in microgravity (8 ISS and 21 Shuttle crew), a change NASA uses for seat and suit fit. | [Young and Rajulu 2020, Appl Ergon](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2019.102995) | high |
| MSN-19 | Spine: in-flight back pain | Space adaptation back pain was recorded in 52% of astronauts (382 of 728), was mild in 86%, moderate in 11% and severe in 3% of those affected, and was relieved most often by a knees-to-chest fetal position (91% effective) or by analgesics or exercise (85% each). | [Kerstman et al. 2012, Aviat Space Environ Med](https://doi.org/10.3357/asem.2876.2012) | high |
| MSN-20 | Spine: disc herniation after flight | The incidence of herniated nucleus pulposus was 4.3 times higher in 321 US astronauts than in 983 matched controls, with a relatively high share in the neck (18 of 44 cases) and the highest risk immediately after spaceflight. | [Johnston et al. 2010, Aviat Space Environ Med](https://doi.org/10.3357/asem.2427.2010) | high |
| MSN-21 | Sensorimotor: space motion sickness | In-flight space motion sickness affects about 70% of crew, develops within hours and usually resolves within 2-3 days, while re-entry motion sickness affects about 30% of crew after 1-2 week flights and 100% after 6-month missions in recent field tests. | [Wood, NASA JSC Neuroscience Laboratory slides (NTRS 20210025094)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf) | medium |
| MSN-22 | Sensorimotor: balance and gait after landing | Nineteen ISS astronauts tested with sit-to-stand, recovery-from-fall, tandem-walk and walk-and-turn tasks a few hours after landing, 1 day later and 6-11 days later showed deficits immediately after landing that lasted up to 1 week, with no difference between first-time and experienced flyers. | [Clement et al. 2022, Front Physiol](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2022.1029161) | high |
| MSN-23 | Sensorimotor: operational rule after a gravity transition | An ISS flight rule schedules no EVA before 72 hours of mission elapsed time because of space motion sickness, and NASA notes that the typical 48 to 72-hour adaptation period will not be practical after landing on short lunar surface missions. | [NASA OCHMO-TB-010 Rev A, Sensorimotor Overview (1 Dec 2023)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-010-sensorimotor.pdf) | high |
| MSN-24 | Sensorimotor: why Mars is different | A Mars crew passes through three gravity fields (about 6 months of weightlessness in transit, about one-third of Earth gravity on the surface, then 1 g on return) with a one-way communication delay of up to about 20 minutes, whereas after Earth landings a flight-surgeon team meets the crew at the landing site and astronauts often need help with basic movements. | [NASA, The Human Body in Space](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space/); [NASA OCHMO-TB-051 (21 May 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ochmo-tb-051-crew-medical-support-entry-descent-landing.pdf) | medium |
| MSN-25 | SANS: definition and prevalence | Spaceflight associated neuro-ocular syndrome is a set of eye findings (optic-disc edema, posterior globe flattening, chorioretinal folds and a hyperopic refractive shift) attributed mainly to the headward fluid shift, and at least one finding was recorded in 52 of 64 NASA astronauts (81%) after long-duration flight between July 2000 and May 2025 (optic-disc edema 69% of 48 evaluated, chorioretinal folds 25% of 64, globe flattening 37% of 43, refractive shift 20% of 45). | [Gew et al. 2026, Eye (NASA-co-authored expert consensus)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6) | high |
| MSN-26 | SANS: what crew notice | Vision degradation was reported by 29% of short-duration and 60% of long-duration crew, measured hyperopic shifts in the first case series ranged from +0.50 to +1.75 dioptres (corrected: the figure in the original paper; the OCHMO brief rounds it to +0.5 to +2.0), every crewmember flies with at least 3 pairs of stronger "space anticipation glasses", and all post-flight vision so far has been correctable to 20/20 or better. | [NASA OCHMO-MTB-001, SANS (21 Feb 2024)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf); [Mader et al. 2011, Ophthalmology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ophtha.2011.06.021) | high |
| MSN-27 | SANS: threshold for the earliest indication | NASA defines the earliest indication of SANS as any one of the following in at least one eye: an increase of 20 micrometres or more in peripapillary total retinal thickness on OCT (normal measurement variability is +/-19.4 micrometres), any chorioretinal folds, any globe flattening, or a refractive shift of +0.75 dioptres or more. | [NASA NTRS 20220014606, SANS 2023 Clinical Update](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220014606); [NASA OCHMO-MTB-001](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf) | high |
| MSN-28 | SANS: threshold for clinically concerning disease | NASA classes SANS as clinically concerning when total retinal thickness has increased by 55 micrometres or more and/or optic-disc edema reaches Frisen grade 1 or higher, when sharp chorioretinal folds lie near the macula, or when globe flattening is moderate, and astronauts who meet the optic-disc threshold are recommended for a lumbar puncture within 7-14 days of return. | [NASA NTRS 20220014606](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220014606) | high |
| MSN-29 | SANS: symptoms that mean functional impact | NASA's "pathological SANS with acute functional impact" tier is visual-field loss such as an enlarged blind spot, distorted central vision, or a refractive shift beyond the power of the glasses on board, and no permanent loss of vision has been recorded in any crewmember to date. | [NASA NTRS 20220014606](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220014606); [Gew et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6) | high |
| MSN-30 | SANS: in-flight monitoring schedule and tools | On 6-month ISS missions NASA examines the eyes before and after flight and on flight days 30 and 90 and 30 days before return, using an ocular questionnaire, visual acuity, Amsler grid, fundoscopy, ocular ultrasound and OCT, with a tonometer on board and visual-field testing added to the ISS in late 2025. | [NASA OCHMO-MTB-001](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf); [NASA NTRS 20220014606](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220014606); [Gew et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6) | high |
| MSN-31 | SANS: countermeasure status | No treatment for SANS is established beyond stronger glasses: in bed-rest studies lower-body negative pressure of 20-25 mmHg was considered safe and 8 hours overnight limited choroid expansion, venoconstrictive thigh cuffs worn for 6 hours after 1 hour of aerobic exercise transiently reduced jugular-vein cross-section, and B-vitamin supplementation is still under study. | [Gew et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6) | high |
| MSN-32 | EVA: in-flight injury rate | A review of the US programme found 219 in-flight musculoskeletal injuries of which 50 came from wearing the EVA suit, equal to 0.26 injuries per EVA (0.05 per hour over 1,087.8 EVA hours), with hand and finger injuries the most common. | [NASA HRP Evidence Report, EVA injury risk (NTRS 20220004017)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220004017/downloads/2021%20EVA%20Injury%20Evidence%20Report%20Final.docx.pdf) | high |
| MSN-33 | EVA: hands and fingernails | In 770 suited pool-training sessions by 86 astronauts, 352 symptoms were reported, spread over 190 sessions (24.6% of sessions, or 0.46 symptoms per session) (corrected: the evidence report's "352, or 45.7%, of the sessions" misquotes the original study), of which 47% involved the hands, 21% the shoulders and 11% the feet, and one analysis put the probability of fingernail injury at 19.6% for hand circumference above 22.86 cm versus 5.6% at or below it, a finding that a later NASA analysis contradicted. | [NASA HRP Evidence Report, EVA injury risk](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220004017/downloads/2021%20EVA%20Injury%20Evidence%20Report%20Final.docx.pdf); [Strauss 2004, NASA/TP-2004-212075](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20060017061) | medium |
| MSN-34 | EVA: shoulders | In a NASA survey of 22 astronauts who trained for EVA in the pool, 64% had shoulder pain they attributed to suited training and 2 needed surgery, while no shoulder injury has been reported in flight. | [NASA OCHMO-MTB-006, Shoulder Injury (4 Sep 2025)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/ochmo-mtb-006-shoulder-injury.pdf) | high |
| MSN-35 | Decompression sickness: symptoms to recognise | NASA classes decompression sickness as Type I (joint or muscle pain, itching, mild rash) or Type II (numbness, weakness, cognitive problems, inner-ear symptoms, cardiopulmonary symptoms and mottled skin called cutis marmorata), most cases appear 60-240 minutes after decompression, and NASA has never had a Type II event in spaceflight. | [NASA OCHMO-TB-037 Rev D, DCS Risk Mitigation (5 Oct 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-037-decompression-sickness.pdf) | high |

## Measurement in space

**Exercise dose and loads (the main in-flight surrogate for bone and muscle)**
- ISS: the ARED records exercise parameters and sends them to the ground; the T2 treadmill records harness load, session duration and speed; CEVIS sessions yield work rate (watts) and heart rate. Strength and conditioning specialists on the ground review the data and adjust the prescription. Typical logged values: T2 on 47% of days for about 27 minutes at 7-8 mph with a harness load averaging 68% of body mass; CEVIS on 37% of days for about 26 minutes at a mean 137 W.
- Exploration: the Orion flywheel sends sensor data to a portable computer that displays feedback in real time and needs no vehicle power for the exercise itself.
- Self-measure: after each session log device, exercise, sets x repetitions x load, treadmill harness load as a percentage of body weight, minutes and average heart rate.
- Cadence: every session, with a weekly roll-up against the 6-days-per-week prescription.

**Aerobic fitness**
- ISS: periodic fitness evaluation on the CEVIS; NASA raises aerobic work rates on the basis of a monthly fitness evaluation and changes in VO2peak. The NASA standards accept "direct or indirect measures".
- Self-measure: repeat the same fixed submaximal workload and record heart rate and perceived effort; a rising heart rate at the same workload suggests falling fitness (a trend indicator, not a calibrated VO2peak).
- Cadence: monthly.

**Muscle strength and size**
- Ground: isokinetic strength testing before and after flight; MRI for muscle size. In flight: load progression on the ARED, and muscle size by a guided ultrasound device.
- Self-measure: track the heaviest load completed for a fixed number of repetitions in squat, deadlift and heel raise and compare with the first weeks of flight.
- Cadence: weekly trend from the exercise log; ultrasound as scheduled.

**Bone**
- No device measures bone density in flight. DXA is done before and after flight (every 3 years for all astronauts; mission-specific scans as in MSN-08; post-flight scans at 6 months and 1, 2 and 3 years or until recovered). QCT of the hip and spine is a supplemental research tool that separates cortical from trabecular bone. Blood and urine bone-turnover markers (bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, resorption markers) have been sampled during flight for research; no on-board assay or operational action threshold was identified in the sources opened.
- Self-measure in flight: only the inputs can be tracked: loading exercise completed, energy intake and body mass, vitamin D supplementation.
- Cadence: inputs daily or weekly; DXA only on the ground.

**Kidney-stone risk**
- Ground: renal ultrasound and 24-hour urine chemistry before and after flight. In flight: ultrasound if a stone is suspected (NASA has tested guided-ultrasound software, AMOS, so that a non-physician can scan the kidneys); urine is collected and analysed to guide diet, exercise and water intake.
- Self-measure: daily fluid intake; note flank pain, blood in urine, nausea.
- Cadence: daily log; ultrasound on symptoms.

**Eyes (SANS)**
- ISS tools: OCT (the key quantitative test), fundoscope, ocular ultrasound for globe flattening, visual-acuity chart, Amsler grid, tonometer, ocular questionnaire, and visual-field testing since late 2025. MRI of brain and orbits and ocular biometry are done on the ground only. Crew are trained before flight (OCT 2.5 hours, fundoscopy 2.5 hours, ultrasound 1 hour, tonometry 1.5 hours for one designated crewmember) and guided from the ground during the exams.
- Self-measure (standard clinical technique, not copied from a NASA procedure, so check against the crew procedure; the Amsler-grid steps were confirmed during verification against the [American Academy of Ophthalmology](https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/facts-about-amsler-grid-daily-vision-test)): test one eye at a time with the usual glasses on; read the smallest line possible on the near and distance charts; look at the centre dot of the Amsler grid and note any wavy, blurred or missing lines; note whether a stronger pair of glasses is now needed.
- Cadence: NASA's schedule for a 6-month mission is flight day 30, flight day 90 and 30 days before return, plus extra testing whenever clinically indicated. No schedule is published for longer missions; continuing OCT at a similar interval and adding a short acuity-and-Amsler self-check every 2 weeks is a Vista proposal.

**Sensorimotor function after a gravity transition**
- NASA field tests: sit-to-stand (time to stabilise after standing), tandem heel-to-toe walk with eyes open and eyes closed (correct steps), walk-and-turn around obstacles (time), and recovery from a simulated fall; computerised posturography on the ground. Performed before flight, a few hours after landing, 1 day later and 6-11 days later.
- Self-measure: with a crewmate spotting and a handhold in reach, repeat the same tasks and compare with the personal preflight baseline; rate motion sickness on the 0-4 scale. Exact step counts and time limits were not confirmed in this run and must come from the NASA test procedure.
- Cadence: baseline before launch; within hours of each transition, at 24 hours, then daily until back at baseline (expect up to 7-11 days).

**Space motion sickness and back pain**
- No device; symptom report. Use the 0-4 severity score once per wake period for the first 72 hours after a transition (motion sickness) and daily in the first week of flight (back pain: record site and severity).

**Spinal lengthening**
- Seated height or stature measurement; matters for suit sizing and seat fit before EVA and before landing (about 6% increase in seated height).

**EVA: decompression sickness and suit injuries**
- DCS is diagnosed from symptoms, not from bubbles. In ground tests venous gas emboli are graded by Doppler or ultrasound on a 0-7 scale as a marker of decompression stress.
- Self-measure: symptom check during the EVA, at suit doffing and repeatedly for 24 hours afterwards (joints, skin, breathing, sensation, strength, balance, thinking); inspect fingernails and skin of the hands and check shoulder movement after every suited session.
- Cadence: every pressure reduction and every suited session.

## Caveats

- **Proposed bands are not NASA values.** NASA publishes single limits (80% aerobic, 80% strength, 95% / 90% BMD) and tiered criteria only for SANS and vitamin D. Every cell tagged "(proposed)" is a design suggestion and needs flight-surgeon review. NASA itself says its fitness values "are not absolute thresholds".
- **SANS prevalence differs by source and date.** 81% with any finding (52 of 64, data to May 2025, 2026 consensus); about 70% with one or more signs and 18% clinically concerning (OCHMO brief, 2024); 66% with the earliest indication, with optic-disc edema 64%, folds 15%, globe flattening 26%, refractive shift 14% (NASA 2023 update). Denominators and definitions differ; the consensus warns that historical data used different methods. Within the consensus paper itself the prevalence table gives optic-disc edema as 69% of 48 astronauts evaluated while the running text says 66% using the 20-micrometre threshold; the table values are used in MSN-25.
- **Refractive-shift threshold differs.** NASA's 2023 update uses +0.75 D or more; the 2026 consensus table gives more than +0.50 D for Class A. The refractive row of the consensus Table 2 was re-read and confirmed (Class A "> +0.50 Dioptres"); the layout of the other rows was partly garbled in the web rendering, so the class structure was cross-checked against the 2023 NASA abstract, which lists the same four tiers.
- **Fluid-pressure thresholds were not included in the rule table.** Lumbar-puncture opening pressure (above 18 cm H2O high-normal, 25 cm H2O or more needing consideration) is a post-flight ground measurement; intracranial pressure has never been measured in flight. No intraocular-pressure threshold for SANS was found.
- **CO2 and SANS.** The consensus reports that headache odds doubled per 1 mmHg rise in CO2 on the ISS but found no association between CO2 and visual changes or SANS; do not link the two in explanations.
- **Bone numbers mix eras.** The 1-1.5% per month figure comes from Mir and early ISS crews without the ARED; the 0.5-1.0% per month figure with current hardware comes from a NASA summary table whose wording is terse. DXA under-detects trabecular loss: hip trabecular bone was lost at 2.2-2.7% per month by QCT, nearly twice the DXA rate for the whole hip.
- **Discrepancy inside NASA documents on the bisphosphonate study.** The 2026 bone brief describes 18 astronauts taking alendronate; the peer-reviewed paper and the exercise brief say 7 took it and were compared with 18 iRED and 11 ARED users. The paper is used here.
- **Bed-rest figures.** The exercise brief estimates 30-day losses without exercise of 17.6% in knee-extension strength and 20.1% in VO2peak, but the caption for the 20.1% figure contains a labelling error, so it was left out of the Facts table.
- **Orion flywheel load.** NASA's Orion page and the Canadian Space Agency give 400 lb (180 kg); a NASA feature published after Artemis II says up to 500 lb depending on effort and gear ratio. The NASA bone brief dated February 2026 still calls Artemis II "upcoming"; the mission flew in April 2026. No physiological results from the flight were found.
- **Gateway.** No source naming the exercise device for Gateway was found; E4D is described as the prototype for "exploration missions" with a recommendation going to the Artemis programme.
- **Partial gravity.** There are no long-duration human data at 1/6 g or 3/8 g. The statement that gravity below 0.4 g is probably insufficient is a review's conclusion from short exposures and simulations. In one simulation tandem walking under visual-vestibular conflict was better at 0.38 g than at 1 g, so balance may be easier on Mars than on return to Earth, but this has not been tested after real spaceflight.
- **Spine.** The 6% figure is seated height (NASA's landing brief also says spinal length "about 6%"); the commonly quoted "up to 3% of standing height" was not confirmed in a source opened in this run. Disc-herniation risk by mission length was not found.
- **Space motion sickness.** The about 70% and 2-3 day figures come from NASA conference slides citing older reviews; a "60-80%" range often quoted elsewhere was not confirmed. A slide pie chart on how many crew completed all field-test tasks 1 hour after landing could not be read unambiguously and was not used.
- **Size of the first reported refractive shifts.** The OCHMO brief quotes +0.5 to +2.0 D for the 2011 case series; the 2026 consensus quotes +0.50 to +1.75 D for the same series. Resolved in verification: the original paper (Mader et al. 2011) gives +0.50 to +1.75 D, so MSN-26 now uses that range.
- **Sensorimotor self-tests.** NASA names the tasks but the exact protocols and pass marks were not confirmed here; NASA-STD-3001 leaves limits to be "operationally defined". The slide label "recovered to 97.5% preflight" does not say whether it means 97.5% of the preflight score or a statistical bound, so it should not be coded as a hard pass mark. A motion-sickness scale of 0-20 mentioned in a 2026 NASA abstract was not opened; the 0-4 landing-assessment scale is used instead.
- **Hands.** Hand-size findings for fingernail injury conflict between studies (larger hand circumference in one, smaller hands and poor glove fit in a later NASA analysis); NASA attributes this to inconsistent injury reporting.
- **Kidney stones.** The brief's stone count is worded "since 2016" although the table spans whole careers. No urine-volume or urine-calcium action threshold was found; calcium intake targets belong to the nutrition domain and were not sourced here.
- **Back-pain Urgent band.** No spaceflight source gave an emergency criterion for back pain; terrestrial red flags (new limb weakness or numbness, loss of bladder or bowel control) should be added from a clinical guideline by whoever owns the medical-care domain.
- **Source access.** The NASA Human Research Roadmap evidence reports for bone, muscle, sensorimotor and SANS (humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov) were not opened; four guessed NASA page addresses returned 404 and were dropped, so the NASA Human Research Program web page for the sensorimotor risk was not read (the OCHMO sensorimotor brief and NASA neuroscience-laboratory slides were used instead). One trade-press page on the SANS consensus returned 403 and was replaced by the paper itself.

### Cautions for the rule engine (not errors of fact)

- NASA's secondary documents carry transcription errors. Four were found in the briefs and reports this file relies on: 45.7% of sessions (really 24.6%), +2.0 D (really +1.75 D), 18 astronauts on alendronate (really 7) and a mislabelled 20.1% caption. Where a brief quotes a paper, use the paper.
- Vitamin D. Do not turn the vitamin D row into an in-flight alert or a dosing prompt. On NASA's own supplement the average crew member measures about 30 ng/mL, inside the 20 to 34 band, so the band would fire constantly, and the doses attached to it are prescriptions above the 4,000 IU/day upper limit. Store the unit with every value: 75 entered as ng/mL would look optimal when it was measured in nmol/L.
- Expected findings. The SANS Watch band (20 micrometres or more) is reached by about two thirds of long-duration crew, so it should read as "expected, keep monitoring" and not as an alarm.
- Cross-file link. The kidney-stone row says no urine-volume target was found; `cardio-metabolic.md` has one (Act below 1.5 L/day, target 2.5 to 3 L/day, from NASA's 2017 renal stone report).
