# Space radiation and the closed habitat environment

_Evidence file for VESTA (Vital Evaluation & Self-Triage Assistant). Compiled 2026-11-14 from live web sources (42 opened). Peer-reviewed papers were retrieved through PubMed / PubMed Central and are linked by DOI. Everything is paraphrased. A number was read from the cited source unless it is marked "derived" or "(P)"._

**Units used in this file**

- **Gy / mGy** is absorbed dose, the quantity a dosimeter physically measures. **mGy-Eq** is absorbed dose weighted for short-term tissue effects. **Sv / mSv** is dose weighted for long-term cancer risk (dose equivalent or effective dose). They are not interchangeable: the measured weighting (quality) factor was 2.3 to 3.1 inside Orion and 4.3 on the lunar surface.
- **pfu** is the proton flux unit (1 proton per cm² per second per steradian).
- **PIO2** is inspired oxygen partial pressure = (cabin pressure in mmHg − 47) × oxygen fraction. A cabin display normally shows dry-gas ppO2, which is higher.
- **LEQ16 / LEQ8** are the average noise level over the 16-hour work period and the 8-hour sleep period.

## Thresholds

**Read this first.** Space agencies publish single limits, not four-colour bands. In the table, **(S)** marks a value taken directly from the cited source and **(P)** marks a VESTA proposal built from sourced anchor points. Every (P) cell needs sign-off from a flight surgeon or radiation health officer before it is shown to a crew member. Cut-points must be configurable per vehicle, because a spacecraft flying the exploration atmosphere runs at a different oxygen and pressure set-point from the ISS.

## Measurement in space

**Personal radiation dose**
- Device: Crew Active Dosimeter (ISS since 2020; one per crew member on Artemis II). Partner agencies also use passive badges read after flight (ESA EuCPD, JAXA Crew-PADLES, Russian passive dosimeters).
- What the crew member sees: an always-on display with two figures, total mission dose and recent dose rate. The device measures absorbed dose, so expect readings in the gray family (ISS typically below 0.3 mGy per day; about 0.4 mGy per day in Orion, derived). Effective dose in mSv is calculated afterwards by NASA's Space Radiation Analysis Group.
- Self-measure steps: keep it in a pocket all day and beside you during sleep; read both figures; enter them in VESTA with the unit shown; let it sync by Bluetooth.
- Cadence: glance once a day; read again straight after any radiation alarm or space-weather alert and when leaving the shelter.
- Check this reading if: a radiation alarm sounds, the ground reports a proton event, or you have unexplained nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite or skin reddening after an event. At doses inside the limits there are no symptoms, so the dosimeter is the only early indicator.

**Area radiation and alarms**
- Devices: ISS-RAD (charged particles and fast neutrons; alarm), REM Timepix units in 6 ISS modules, HERA on Orion (dose every minute, caution-and-warning), DLR M-42 EXT (4 units on Artemis II), ESA Active Dosimeter (Artemis I).
- Crew steps: none in normal operation; on alarm, follow the shelter procedure and note the time.
- Cadence: continuous and automatic. NASA's Acute Radiation Risks Tool uses the onboard dosimeter data to project organ doses and the likelihood of acute symptoms.

**Neutrons**
- Devices: bubble detectors (Radi-N2 method) and, in development, the Canadian Active Neutron Spectrometer (continuous and autonomous).
- Steps with bubble detectors: read all eight detectors in the automatic reader for a baseline, place six at fixed locations, wear one and put one in the sleep station, collect after seven days, read again, record the bubble counts.
- Reading and cadence: bubble counts converted on the ground to neutron dose equivalent in µSv; one-week sessions.

**Space weather**
- Source: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center alerts passed to NASA's Space Radiation Analysis Group, supported by the Moon to Mars Space Weather Analysis Office at Goddard.
- Crew steps: check the proton-storm level before each EVA; avoid low-shielded areas when advised.
- Cadence: continuous on the ground; a daily summary on board is sensible (P).

**Cabin CO2**
- Device: the vehicle's atmosphere sensors, which NASA requires to record and display ppCO2 in real time in every isolatable compartment and to alarm when out of limits. On the ISS the scrubbers are the CDRA and the Russian Vozdukh.
- Steps: read ppCO2 on the environmental display; note the 1-hour and 24-hour averages; if the value is normal but symptoms persist, check airflow at the place where you sleep or work, because pockets form where ventilation is weak.
- Cadence: automatic (example rate every 5 seconds); crew review once a day and whenever symptoms appear.
- Check this reading if: headache (especially frontal), unusual fatigue or malaise, poor sleep, blurred vision, persistent cough, nausea, facial tingling, or breathlessness.

**Oxygen and total pressure**
- Device: the vehicle's pressure and ppO2 sensors (real-time display and alarm required).
- Steps: read total pressure and ppO2; VESTA converts to PIO2; compare with the vehicle set-point.
- Cadence: automatic (pressure every second in NASA's example); crew review daily and before and after any planned pressure change.
- Check this reading if: fatigue, nausea, headache, dizziness, mood change, dimmer colour, night or side vision, fast breathing, or a crewmate who seems confused; ear or sinus pain during a pressure change. Crew are trained to put on an oxygen mask at the first hypoxia symptom.

**Temperature and humidity**
- Device: cabin sensors (example rate every minute).
- Steps: read both values together; adjust the set-point, airflow or clothing.
- Cadence: daily review.
- Check this reading if: dry, stuffy or irritated nose, throat or eyes, static shocks (low humidity); skin rash or condensation (high humidity); feeling overheated or chilled.

**Noise**
- Device: Acoustic Monitor (a Svantek SV 102A+ dosimeter that also works as a sound level meter); three are kept on the ISS.
- Steps: clip the unit to clothing with the microphone on the collar near the ear; wear it for 24 hours; change the battery just before sleep; download by USB; on another day take a short survey of fixed locations.
- Cadence: about every other month on the ISS; sooner after new equipment or a complaint.
- Check this reading if: you must raise your voice to be understood, miss alarms or calls, feel irritable or get headaches in a noisy module, or sleep badly.

**Dust (lunar or Martian)**
- Device: a particulate monitor reporting particle size and mass concentration; NASA expects it to be used situationally, for example to confirm the cabin has cleared after an EVA.
- Steps: start the monitor at ingress; log the reading until it returns to baseline; clean suits before entry and vacuum surfaces.
- Cadence: every EVA ingress (example sampling every minute while running).
- Check this reading if: gritty or irritated eyes, sneezing, nasal congestion, cough, or skin abrasion under gloves or fingernails.

**Water quality**
- Devices: the Total Organic Carbon Analyzer for the US water processor, in-flight microbial sampling (plate count and coliform test), and archive samples returned to Earth for full chemistry.
- Steps: draw the sample from the dispenser as trained; run the analyser or incubate the plate; record the result and the dispenser used.
- Cadence: UNVERIFIED: total organic carbon about weekly (reported practice taken from a search snippet; no opened source gives the interval); microbial cadence not confirmed (see Caveats).
- Check this reading if: the water tastes or smells odd, or anyone has vomiting, diarrhoea or fever. Check your intake if urine output falls or you have headache, poor concentration or sleepiness (NASA's dehydration signs; minimum 2.5 L of water per day).

**Air and surface microbes**
- Devices: air sampler and surface swabs grown on culture media; DNA sequencing (MinION) and miniPCR have been tested on the ISS for identification.
- Steps: swab a 25 cm² area or run the air sampler; incubate; count colonies; photograph the plate.
- Cadence: routine on the ISS, exact interval not confirmed; also after visible growth, a leak or an infection.
- Check this reading if: visible mould or slime, a musty smell, skin infection, boils or rash, or several crew members with the same illness.

## Caveats

- **Units cannot be mixed.** Dosimeters report absorbed dose (mGy); the limits are in mSv or mGy-Eq. The conversion depends on the radiation field (quality factor 2.3–3.1 in Orion, 4.3 on the Moon), so VESTA must not compare a raw mGy reading with an mSv limit. NASA's "effective dose" also uses NASA-specific quality factors, so NASA and partner-agency mSv values are not strictly the same quantity.
- **The four-band scheme is ours.** Agencies give one limit per quantity. Every cell marked (P), including the 50 % and 80 % dose fractions and the 2-times and 10-times dose-rate multipliers, is a placeholder that needs expert sign-off. The real alarm set-points of ISS-RAD and HERA and NASA's radiation flight-rule action levels were not found in public sources.
- **ISS six-month dose disagrees between sources.** The older NASA educator figure is 80–160 mSv. The 2024 review figure of 0.2–0.5 mSv per day gives about 36–91 mSv over 182 days (derived). The 2019 point measurement of 731 µSv per day would give about 133 mSv (derived), but that is dose equivalent at one spot, not effective dose.
- **Deep-space rates depend on shielding and solar cycle.** MSL-RAD's 1.8 mSv per day (2011–2012) and Orion's 0.96–1.24 mSv per day (2022) are both correct for their vehicle and date. The Artemis I paper quotes 1.58 mSv per day for MSL-RAD at a comparable point in the solar cycle. The lunar figure was taken behind about 1 g/cm², similar to a spacesuit, not a habitat.
- **Mars surface number.** The instrument team's press release says 0.67 mSv per day; reports of the peer-reviewed paper (Scientific American, 9 December 2013) and a later review table give 0.64 ± 0.12 mSv per day on the surface, 1.84 mSv per day in cruise and about 1.01 Sv for the 180 + 500 + 180-day mission, so RAD-06 now uses 0.64 (changed at verification). The paper's full text was still not opened; the journal abstract opened does not print the rate. Search snippets of the paper gave 0.21 mGy per day on the surface and 0.48 mGy per day in cruise, which were not verified against the full text.
- **Editions.** Volume 1 Revision C (2023) is the newest edition found, and NASA's standards page still lists it. Volume 2 Revision F was read from a "tmp" path on standards.nasa.gov, so use the NASA standards page as the stable link. The radiation brief the researchers opened was Revision E (2022), which cites Volume 1 Revision B; the current brief is Revision G (2025-06-06), cites Volume 1 Revision C and repeats the same shielding, shelter and EVA figures, and the links in this file now point to it. One summary of the Shavers review listed NASA's one-year blood-forming-organ limit as 600 mGy-Eq; the standard itself says 500 mGy-Eq and that value is used here.
- **Partner limits.** JAXA and Roscosmos short-term limits were not confirmed, and JAXA's table may have been revised since the 2024 review. The CSA organ-limit page is dated 2006.
- **CO2 evidence is partly anecdotal.** The symptom levels come from crew reports. Ground studies cited by NASA found no clear loss of cognition at spacecraft CO2 levels, and NASA notes that headward fluid shift in weightlessness produces similar headaches. There are no official off-nominal CO2 limits since the SMACs were withdrawn.
- **Exploration atmosphere.** The PIO2 of about 128 mmHg for 8.2 psia and 34 % O2 is our calculation from NASA's formula. NASA states that long-duration effects at 127 mmHg in weightlessness are unknown and requires a monitoring plan.
- **Temperature and humidity envelope.** The researchers could read only the two corner points and the office-comfort range from the text. At verification the two figures were extracted from the Revision F file: the health envelope (Figure 6.2-2) has four corners, 18 °C / 75 %, 25.7 °C / 75 %, 27 °C / 25 % and 19.1 °C / 25 %, and the performance zone (Figure 6.2-3) is 20–25 °C by 30–60 %. The Thresholds row now carries the four corners.
- **Noise.** The A-weighted equivalents of the NC-50 and NC-40 curves were not extracted, so the table uses the ISS flight-rule values. The ISS flight-rule text itself is not public; the values come from a NASA conference paper (2021).
- **Microbial limits table.** The column layout of the brief was scrambled in extraction; the assignment of values to air, surface and water follows the units printed. Sampling cadence for water and surface microbes on the ISS was not found. Weekly total-organic-carbon monitoring comes from a search snippet, not an opened document.
- **Shelter procedure.** The Orion description dates from 2016. Trade press reported that the Artemis II shelter demonstration was shortened; this was not confirmed in a NASA source, and Artemis II crew dose totals have not been published.
- **Warning time is not a fixed number.** NOAA gives arrival in tens of minutes or less; NASA guidance says event timelines normally leave time to reconfigure. No source gives a guaranteed lead time.
- **CSA article.** It names no health study, no flight date for CANS and no dose values, and it does not state the currency of the $5.5 million contract. It does not mention Artemis or Gateway.
- **Not confirmed:** effectiveness of the AstroRad vest flown on Artemis I; the units printed on the Crew Active Dosimeter display; exact titles of all current Human Research Program radiation risks; the UNSCEAR world-average background figure (page blocked).
