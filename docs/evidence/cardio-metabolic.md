# Cardiovascular system, fluids, nutrition, metabolism and kidneys

Evidence file for VESTA (Vital Evaluation & Self-Triage Assistant). Compiled 2026-11-14 from live web sources (NASA, CSA, guideline bodies) and from peer-reviewed abstracts retrieved through PubMed (every paper is linked by DOI). Facts are paraphrased. "Confidence" rates how directly the opened source supports the sentence, not how large the underlying study was; sample sizes are stated in the claim so the reader can judge. Anything marked (P) is a VESTA proposal, not a number taken from a source.

## Thresholds

How to read this table. (S) marks a band taken directly from the cited source. (P) marks a VESTA proposal that maps NASA or guideline numbers onto the four colours where no source defines a band. For heart rate, blood pressure and SpO2 the bands follow the same rule as `docs/evidence/clinical-thresholds.md`: NEWS2 per-parameter score 0 = Nominal, 1 = Watch, 2 = Act, 3 = Urgent, cross-checked against the specialist guideline. Repeat any single abnormal reading once, after correct preparation, before changing colour, unless a red-flag symptom is present. All cut-offs are terrestrial values for general adults; none has been validated in astronauts.

Expected and benign versus act, in one line per indicator:

- Resting heart rate. Expected: no change or a few beats/min lower in flight; about 5 beats/min higher for some days after landing. Act: 111 beats/min or more at rest, or a new irregular pulse.
- Blood pressure. Expected: 24-hour values about 8–10 mmHg lower in flight; light-headedness on standing in the first days after landing. Act: 140/90 mmHg or more on repeat, anything above 180/120 mmHg, or systolic 100 mmHg or less with symptoms; systolic 90 mmHg or less confirmed on repeat is urgent even without symptoms (corrected).
- SpO2. Expected: no change in a sea-level-equivalent cabin. Act: 93% or less confirmed on repeat; 91% or less is urgent.
- Body mass. Expected: an early loss of roughly 3–4% within the first 30 days (only about 0.5–0.7% of body mass of that is water, because total body water falls by about 1%; corrected), then stable. Act: 5% or more below preflight; still falling after day 30 is a Watch sign.
- Fluid intake. Expected: less thirst and a tendency to drink less (common, not benign). Act: below 2.0 L/day for 2 days or more.
- Urine colour. Expected: nothing specific to spaceflight. Act: colour 5 or more on repeat; red, pink or cola-coloured urine is urgent.
- Fasting glucose. Expected: a small upward drift late in a long mission is plausible. Act: 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or more on repeat, or below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L).
- Energy intake. Expected: appetite changes (in one Russian report 40% of Mir crew ate with less appetite, 40% unchanged, 20% more). Act: weekly mean below 80% of requirement, or any shortfall with 5% body-mass loss.

## Measurement in space

Cadences below are VESTA proposals (P) unless a source is named, because the routine ISS medical-exam schedule could not be confirmed from a source I opened.

**Resting heart rate**
- On the ISS: ECG and Holter recorders (repeated 48-hour Holter sessions were used in the atrial study, CVM-11), the CSA Bio-Monitor shirt (CVM-17), and the pulse value shown by a pulse oximeter. NASA lists "assess and store vital signs" and "perform cardiac waveform monitoring" among the in-mission capabilities a mission may need ([OCHMO-TB-033](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/ochmo-tb-033-spaceflight-experience-medical-care-rev-c.pdf)).
- Self-measure: rest quietly for 5 minutes; place the index and middle fingers on the underside of the other wrist just below the base of the thumb; count beats for 30 seconds and double it, or count a full minute if the rhythm feels uneven ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm)). Never press on both sides of the neck at once.
- Cadence (P): once a day on waking, at the same point in the schedule; again whenever there are palpitations, dizziness or chest symptoms.

**Blood pressure**
- On the ISS: automatic arm cuffs; research has used 24-hour ambulatory brachial monitors reading every 1–2 hours (CVM-05), continuous finger arterial pressure (Hughson 2012), the ESA Cardiolab leg/arm cuff system ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/space-health-research/cardiovascular-health-in-microgravity/)), and the Bio-Monitor, which lists blood pressure among its outputs (CVM-17).
- Self-measure: rest 5 minutes; cuff on the bare upper arm, arm relaxed and still; take two readings one minute apart and log the average; avoid measuring within 30 minutes of exercise or caffeine (confirmed during verification against [CDC, Measure Your Blood Pressure](https://www.cdc.gov/high-blood-pressure/measure/index.html): no smoking, alcohol, caffeine or exercise in the 30 minutes before, at least 5 minutes of seated rest, cuff on the bare upper arm, at least two readings 1 or 2 minutes apart). The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline recommends home monitoring to confirm and track high readings.
- Cadence (P): weekly in flight; daily for a week after any Watch or Act reading; a 24-hour profile early and mid-mission where an ambulatory monitor exists; lying and standing readings under supervision in the first days after landing.

**SpO2**
- On the ISS: fingertip pulse oximeter in the medical kit (not confirmed from a NASA source I opened) and the Bio-Monitor sensor (CVM-17).
- Self-measure, from the WHO manual: use a clean, warm finger with no nail varnish; do not use the arm carrying the blood-pressure cuff; keep still and shield the probe from bright light; wait several seconds for a steady pulse signal; if the hand is cold, rub the finger and repeat.
- Cadence (P): weekly spot check; at once with breathlessness, headache or after any cabin-atmosphere alarm.

**Body mass**
- On the ISS: SLAMMD in Columbus (springs apply a known force, acceleration is measured, mass = force / acceleration) and the Russian BMMD in Zvezda (spring oscillation) (CVM-22).
- Self-measure: always use the same device, follow the device procedure, repeat the run and log the average, then convert to % change from the preflight scale weight. Expect the two devices to disagree by about 1 kg.
- Cadence (P): every 2 weeks for the first 2 months, when loss occurs, then monthly.

**Fluid and energy intake**
- On the ISS: the ISS Food Intake Tracker (ISS FIT) iPad app, deployed in August 2016, followed an earlier weekly food-frequency questionnaire; it shows intake in real time and feeds weekly reports to flight surgeons on energy, fluid, calcium, protein, sodium, iron and potassium. Through 2020 it had been used on more than 3,500 crew-days by 22 astronauts with data on more than 89% of days ([NASA nutrition book](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/human_adaptation_2021_final.pdf)).
- Self-measure: log each food and drink package when it is opened; compare the 7-day mean with the personal energy requirement (NASA uses the DRI estimated-energy-requirement equations with an activity factor, [OCHMO-TB-013](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-013-food-and-nutrition.pdf)) and with 32 mL of fluid per kg per day.
- Cadence: log daily; review weekly, which matches the flight-surgeon reporting cycle (S).

**Urine colour and volume**
- On the ISS: 24-hour urine collections for a renal-stone risk profile are a medical requirement before and after flight; in-flight collections ran from 2006 and stopped in 2018. Crew-operated ultrasound with remote guidance can show stones smaller than 3 mm, and NASA began post-flight renal ultrasound within 30 days of landing in February 2016 ([NASA renal stone report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170004709/downloads/20170004709.pdf); [NASA nutrition book](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/human_adaptation_2021_final.pdf)).
- Self-measure: compare a fresh sample with the 8-colour chart under white light; aim for 1–3. How easily urine colour can be seen with spacecraft urine-collection hardware was not confirmed.
- Cadence (P): first sample of each day; a 24-hour volume estimate when colour 4 or more persists.

**Fasting glucose**
- On the ISS: no routine glucose monitoring was found in the NASA sources opened. Glucose is measured in research (CVM-33) and continuous glucose monitors were flown on Axiom Mission 4 (CVM-34).
- Self-measure: after at least 8 hours with water only, on waking; clean, dry finger for a fingerstick meter, or read the sensor value.
- Cadence (P): monthly fasting check; repeat on another day after any Watch value.

**Neck veins and heart rhythm (crew-operated exams, not self-triage)**
- Duplex ultrasound of both internal jugular veins with breathing manoeuvres at three points in flight under NASA's surveillance programme (CVM-16); 48-hour Holter recording for rhythm questions (CVM-11).

## Caveats

- Small samples. Most spaceflight numbers come from 4 to 13 astronauts (cardiac atrophy 4, arterial stiffness and insulin resistance 8, ambulatory blood pressure 8 men, cardiac MRI 13, jugular study 11). Treat them as typical directions and magnitudes, not as limits for an individual.
- NASA sources disagree on early diuresis. The NASA nutrition book (2021) says in-flight diuresis and dehydration have never been confirmed and that urine volume is lower in flight; NASA's OCHMO-TB-033 brief describes "temporary diuresis with polyuria" and a 12–15% volume reduction affecting all crew. VESTA should not tell crew that heavy early urination is expected; the measured data favour the nutrition book.
- Orthostatic intolerance percentages depend on the test and the era: about 20–25% after short flights and 67–83% after long flights in tilt tests, versus NASA's summary figure of "more than 60% without countermeasures". None of these is a rate for today's crews using all countermeasures.
- Stone counts differ by source and date: 14 episodes in 12 astronauts in an older survey, 37 symptomatic events as of 2016 in the 2017 evidence report, and 43 events in 29 people as of December 2020 in the nutrition book. The 2024 update of the evidence report exists but its PDF was too large to read, so newer counts and probabilities were not checked.
- Body mass. The two ISS devices differ by about 1.1 kg and both are compared with a preflight scale weight, so percentage loss is device dependent (4.4% versus 2.8% in the same people).
- Thresholds are terrestrial. NEWS2 was built to detect acute illness in hospital patients, the AHA/ACC categories describe long-term cardiovascular risk, and the ADA and CDC glucose levels were written for diagnosing or managing diabetes with plasma glucose, not for self-monitoring by healthy astronauts with fingerstick or interstitial readings. The (P) bands are proposals for the team to review with a flight surgeon.
- Alignment with `docs/evidence/clinical-thresholds.md`. Heart rate, blood pressure, SpO2, glucose, urine colour and body mass were kept consistent with that file. Two rows differ on purpose: (1) fluid intake here scales with body mass (32 mL/kg/day, minimum 2.1 L for women and 2.5 L for men, from the NASA nutrition requirement) whereas that file uses a flat 2.5 L/day; (2) urine volume here is a stone-prevention rule (target 2.5–3 L, Act below 1.5 L, from NASA's renal evidence report) whereas that file uses the general clinical range (800–2,000 mL, Act below 500 mL). The rule engine should carry both urine rules under different names rather than merge them.
- Blood pressure sources. The category cut-offs up to stage 2 come from the AHA's own news release; the rows above 180/120 mmHg come from a Harvard Health summary because heart.org refused automated access and the guideline full text was not opened. The AHA release dates the guideline 14 August 2025; Harvard describes it as the October 2025 issue of Hypertension.
- Glucose sources. The ADA 2026 Standards of Care full text could not be read, so cut-offs come from the ADA and CDC public pages; mmol/L values are conversions (mg/dL divided by 18). No source says glucose is routinely monitored in flight.
- Continuous glucose monitors. The only in-orbit evidence found is a sponsor press release without data, device names or peer review. In a centrifuge study of 26 volunteers the monitors were generally reliable but gave four false critical-low alarms, so alarms from such a sensor should be confirmed by fingerstick before the engine escalates.
- Insulin resistance. The abstract reports an "index of insulin resistance" without naming it or giving its size, and the CSA page gives no numbers for the later Vascular Echo and Vascular Aging studies.
- Heart-rate direction. The Shuttle paper reports a significant in-flight fall in heart rate but its abstract gives no magnitude; ISS studies report no change.
- Jugular stasis may not need long exposure: flow stasis appeared in the left jugular vein of 1 of 13 volunteers during parabolic flight.
- SpO2 assumes sea-level-equivalent cabin air. Exploration vehicles may use lower-pressure, oxygen-enriched atmospheres; the expected baseline there was not sourced.
- Urine colour charts were validated on Earth (pregnant, lactating and control women; young men exercising in heat). Supplements can change urine colour, and visibility of urine in spacecraft collection hardware was not confirmed.
- Not confirmed and therefore left out or marked (P): the ISS routine medical-exam schedule, the make and location of the ISS pulse oximeter and cuff, Bio-Monitor wear time per session, SLAMMD accuracy (a figure of 0.5 lb appears only in secondary copies), fluid-loading volumes and compression-garment pressures, exercise minutes per day, and the American Urological Association urine-volume recommendation (the guideline page is behind a login).

### Cautions for the rule engine (not errors of fact)

- Alarm fatigue. The proposed body-mass Act band (5% or more) was reached by 57% of ISS crews. Urine volume about 17% below a preflight mean of 2.1 L/day is about 1.7 L/day, so the average crew member sits in the Watch band of the urine-volume row. The NEWS2 101–110 mmHg band is an ordinary resting value for many small, fit adults, so the baseline rule should also apply before and after flight. The CDC "below 70 mg/dL" level was written for people treated for diabetes.
- Cross-file conflict on vitamin D. This file says not to exceed 4,000 IU/day; `musculoskeletal-neuro.md` lists NASA's repletion doses of 5,000 IU a day or 50,000 IU a week. Those are short pre-mission prescriptions by a crew surgeon; NASA's nutrition book reports that 50,000 IU weekly for 4 weeks then monthly raised urinary calcium outside the normal range, which matters for stone risk. The app must not present them as self-care.
- Edition risk. The 1.5 and 2.5–3 L/day urine figures come from a 2016 model quoted in the 2017 report; the 2024 report that supersedes it was not readable.
